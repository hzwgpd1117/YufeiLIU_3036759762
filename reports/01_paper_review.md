# Paper review: Jegadeesh and Titman (1993)

**Reference.** Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman, “Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency,” *Journal of Finance* 48(1), 65–91, 1993. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb04702.x); [readable copy of the paper](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/rsusmel/phd/jegadeesh-titman93.pdf). This review was checked against the 28-page scanned copy supplied with the project and the readable copy linked above. Page numbers below refer to the journal's printed pages.

## Research question and economic nature

The paper asks whether a stock's relative return over the previous three to twelve months predicts its return over the next three to twelve months. Its broader economic question is whether security prices fully and promptly incorporate information. If past winners continue to outperform past losers after accounting for risk and trading frictions, past prices contain information about future returns that a simple efficient-market account does not explain.

This is an **empirical asset-pricing and market-efficiency study**. The economic object is a self-financing relative-strength strategy: buy stocks with high past returns and sell stocks with low past returns. The paper distinguishes several possible sources of its return: persistent differences in expected returns across firms, predictable returns on a common risk factor, delayed reactions to common-factor news, and persistence in firm-specific return components. Positive portfolio returns alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms. The authors' interpretation emphasizes delayed incorporation of firm-specific information, while their later evidence of reversal allows more than one behavioral explanation (pp. 71–76, 83–90).

The paper is observational. It tests predictions of competing return-generating stories; it does not randomly assign information or identify a unique causal psychological mechanism. In particular, the authors acknowledge that temporary price pressure and a combination of short-run underreaction and longer-run overreaction could both fit part of the evidence (p. 90).

## Economic method: how the paper turns the question into tests

1. **Portfolio sorting.** At the beginning of each month, stocks are ranked on their compounded return over the previous **J** months. The bottom decile is the loser portfolio; the top decile is the winner portfolio. The strategy buys winners and sells losers for **K** months, with J and K each taking 3, 6, 9, or 12 months. A new vintage is formed each month, so the K active vintages overlap. Stocks are equally weighted within each decile and rebalanced monthly. The paper also forms portfolios with a one-week gap between the ranking window and holding period to reduce very short-run price effects (pp. 68–70).
2. **Risk and return decomposition.** A one-factor return model separates differences in unconditional expected returns, time dependence in the common factor, and serial dependence in firm-specific returns. A second model permits lagged reactions to the common factor. These models organize the interpretation of profits; the decile spread by itself is not a direct estimate of every theoretical component (pp. 70–76).
3. **Characteristics and within-group tests.** The authors compare the ten 6/6 portfolios' post-ranking market betas and average firm sizes (Table II). They then sort stocks into size and *ex ante* beta groups before repeating the winner–loser sort within each group (Table III). This checks whether the signal is merely sorting firms by familiar risk characteristics (pp. 72–78).
4. **Timing and information tests.** The paper examines calendar-month and five-year subsamples, follows portfolios for 36 months after formation, and studies returns around subsequent earnings announcements. The long event-time path asks whether initial momentum later reverses; earnings dates help connect return predictability to the release of firm-level information (pp. 79–89).
5. **Implementability.** The authors discuss turnover and an illustrative transaction-cost adjustment. This matters because a positive pre-cost spread need not be an attainable investor return (p. 77).

## Statistical method: how evidence is measured

The original sample comprises NYSE and AMEX stocks from January 1965 through December 1989. The authors compound CRSP daily returns into monthly returns (p. 69). For each J/K specification, Table I reports average monthly returns of the loser, winner, and winner-minus-loser portfolios, along with t statistics. The authors explicitly consider the 32 related strategy tests when discussing the strongest t statistic, using a Bonferroni bound (pp. 69–70). These strategies share stocks and calendar months, so their results are not 32 independent replications.

Table II estimates post-ranking betas against a value-weighted market index and summarizes average market capitalizations. Table III reports raw portfolio means and t statistics, then estimates market-model intercepts (CAPM-style alphas) from portfolio excess returns on market excess returns. It also reports joint F tests of equal decile means and of zero alphas. The beta groups use Scholes–Williams betas estimated from daily returns in the calendar year before portfolio formation (pp. 73–78).

The paper further examines serial covariance in market and firm-specific returns and uses a time-series regression relating momentum profits to the square of the previous market return to assess a common-factor lead–lag account (pp. 73–76). For cumulative event-time returns based on overlapping portfolios, it explicitly uses Newey–West autocorrelation-consistent standard errors (p. 83). The paper does not state that every t statistic in Tables I–III uses the same Newey–West specification; our replication's HAC lag choice is therefore an implementation decision, not a claim of exact statistical identity.

## Main findings and interpretation

All 32 winner-minus-loser means in Table I are positive in the original sample, and all but one are statistically significant under the paper's reported tests. The largest spread is the 12/3 strategy: 1.31% per month with immediate formation and 1.49% with the one-week gap. The representative 6/6 strategy earns about 0.95% per month in Table III's all-stock row (pp. 69–70, 78).

Table II shows a post-ranking beta of 1.36 for losers and 1.28 for winners, implying a winner-minus-loser beta of −0.08. Losers are also smaller than winners on average. Under a positive market risk premium, buying lower-beta winners and selling higher-beta losers does not provide a simple high-beta explanation for the positive spread (p. 73). Table III finds positive spreads within size and prior-beta groups and a market-adjusted all-stock spread of about 1.00% per month. The paper interprets this as evidence against simple market-risk and size accounts, while its additional factor tests provide evidence against a delayed reaction to common-market news (pp. 76–78).

Momentum is not permanent in the paper's event-time analysis: the cumulative winner-minus-loser spread reaches about 9.5% after 12 months and falls to about 4% by month 36. Earnings-announcement returns also favor previous winners early and previous losers later. These patterns are consistent with gradual response to firm news followed by some reversal, but the paper cannot uniquely separate underreaction, overreaction, and price-pressure mechanisms (pp. 83–90).

## Critical assessment and link to this project

The paper's strength is its sequence of tests: it establishes a return pattern, checks common risk and size explanations, examines when returns occur, and asks whether trading costs matter. Its limits are also important. Portfolio sorts and market-model alphas are conditional on a particular risk model; they do not prove market inefficiency or an investor-behavior mechanism. Later reversal estimates are imprecise, and shorting, trading costs, and changes in market structure affect implementability and historical comparisons.

Our course project reproduces Tables I–III on supplied 2000–2025 data, with a common 2002–2025 reporting window. Its 6/6 raw spread is **0.670% per month (HAC t = 1.32)**, versus about **0.95%** in the paper. Its winner-minus-loser beta is approximately **−0.635**, and its CAPM alpha is **1.130% per month (HAC t = 2.85)**. The sign of the spread and the negative market-beta pattern resemble the original results, but the weaker raw-return t statistic does not reproduce the original strength of evidence. The [replication report](02_replication.md) now gives an original-versus-modern 6/6 table with the distinct sample periods and inference labels. Our Table I one-week gap is approximated with five daily observations; our HAC inference, CRSP CIZ data, and modern sample also differ from the historical design. See the [validation notes](06_validation_and_ai.md).

The Mom-factor comparison checks construction against a public benchmark. The short- and long-horizon reversal analyses ask related questions, but our 36/36 reversal sort is not the paper's Table VII event-time follow-up of the **same 6/6 momentum portfolios**. An [additional analysis in the reversal extension](04_extension_reversal.md) follows monthly cohorts ranked on the previous six months for 36 months after formation. Its 12- and 36-month cumulative spreads are **2.86** and **2.30 percentage points**, respectively, with wide HAC intervals that include zero. This is a modern-sample analogue of Table VII's question, not a reproduction of its historical estimate or conclusive evidence of reversal.

The [market-state extension](05_extension_market_state.md) uses the same Table I 6/6 monthly spread and asks whether its return differs after a negative prior-year market return. The mean is **1.456%** per month after nonnegative prior-year market returns and **−2.025%** after negative ones. The difference is suggestive but its HAC 95% interval includes zero (two-sided p = **0.069**). Positive same-month market returns following a decline coincide with especially weak momentum returns in this sample; that same-month split is descriptive, not a usable advance signal. This extension adds a conditional-risk question rather than another replication of the original tables.

## Remaining priorities before submission

1. **Assess implementability and concentration.** Estimate portfolio turnover and a clearly labeled range of transaction-cost assumptions; report the return contribution from the largest positive and negative months and from major subperiods. These checks are especially useful when a raw t statistic is modest.
2. **Strengthen claims about heterogeneity.** Test the *difference* between small- and large-stock spreads and between high- and low-beta spreads. Separate group t statistics do not establish that their spreads differ significantly.
3. **Tighten the data and inference audit.** State market-capitalization units; verify delisting-return treatment and a few daily-to-monthly examples; document the five-trading-observation gap precisely; and label the validation count of nonmissing beta estimates as security-years rather than calendar years. Keep the restricted CRSP files out of the public repository.

These are priorities for improving the current coursework package, not claims that the paper itself performed every proposed check.
